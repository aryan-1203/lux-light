A bright light lamp is a simple thing. You switch it on, sit near it and carry on with your morning. For most healthy adults, that is all there is to it.

But "simple" is not the same as "right for everyone". Bright light has real effects on the body, which is the whole point. This guide covers who should check with a doctor first and what to watch for. It is not medical advice, and it does not replace a conversation with your doctor.

## First, what the lamp is and is not

The lamp is a wellness product. It is designed to support mood, energy and a regular daily routine. It is **not a medical device**. It is not intended to diagnose, treat or manage any condition, and we make no such claim.

Researchers have studied bright light in a range of settings, including in the context of seasonal low mood and sleep timing. That research is interesting, but it is done under medical supervision with specific protocols. If you are thinking about light for a health reason, the right first step is a doctor, not a lamp.

## Who should check with a doctor first

Please speak to a doctor before using the lamp if any of these apply to you.

### Bipolar disorder

Bright light can affect mood and energy. In people with bipolar disorder, there are reports of it contributing to elevated or agitated mood. If you have bipolar disorder, or a close family history of it, please do not start a light routine without your doctor's guidance.

### Eye conditions

If you have, or have had, any of the following, talk to an eye doctor first:

- Retinal conditions, including macular degeneration or diabetic eye disease
- Glaucoma
- Cataracts, or recent cataract surgery
- Any other ongoing eye condition or recent eye surgery

The lamp is UV-free, but bright visible light can still be unsuitable for eyes that need extra care. Older adults may have conditions such as cataracts or glaucoma without knowing it, so a quick eye check before starting is a good idea.

### Medication that increases light sensitivity

Some medicines make the eyes or skin more sensitive to light. These are called photosensitising medications. They can include certain antibiotics, acne medicines, anti-inflammatory drugs, heart medicines and some herbal supplements.

If you take any regular medication, ask your doctor or pharmacist whether it increases light sensitivity before you start.

### Other situations worth a conversation

- You are pregnant or breastfeeding.
- You have a history of migraines that are triggered by light.
- You are under 18. The lamp is designed for adults, so please do not use it with children.
- You have any condition you are already seeing a doctor for, and you are unsure.

<Note>
  If you are feeling persistently low, hopeless or unable to cope, please reach out to a doctor or a trusted person
  today. A lamp is not the right tool for that moment. In India, you can contact a national mental health helpline, or
  go to your nearest hospital in an emergency.
</Note>

## Side effects to watch for

Most people notice nothing, or only mild effects in the first few days. Watch for:

- Headaches
- Eye strain, dryness or discomfort
- Feeling irritable or on edge
- Feeling "wired", restless or overly energetic
- Trouble falling asleep at night
- Mild nausea

If any of these happen, stop using the lamp and talk to a doctor. Often a shorter session, lower brightness, more distance or an earlier time of day is enough. But if symptoms are strong, or they continue after you stop, please get them checked.

## How to use the lamp sensibly

1. **Never stare into the lamp.** Place it off to one side so the light reaches your eyes at an angle. Read, eat or work as usual.
2. **Keep a sensible distance.** Sit about 8–24 inches (20–60 cm) away.
3. **Keep sessions short.** 20–30 minutes is typical. Use the auto-off timer.
4. **Use it in the morning.** Avoid bright light late in the evening, as it can make it harder to wind down and fall asleep.
5. **Start gently.** Use a lower brightness level or the far end of the distance range for the first week.
6. **Listen to your body.** Comfort is the guide. If something feels off, stop.

Night-shift workers can use it near the start of their shift instead.

## A word on UV and vitamin D

The lamp uses UV-free LEDs. That means it does not expose your skin or eyes to ultraviolet light. It also means it does **not** produce vitamin D. If you are concerned about your vitamin D levels, talk to your doctor about testing and the right approach for you.

## The short version

- It is a wellness lamp, not a medical device.
- Check with a doctor first if you have bipolar disorder, an eye condition or take photosensitising medication.
- Stop and talk to a doctor if you get headaches, eye strain, irritability or feel wired.
- Sit to one side, never stare, keep it to the morning.
